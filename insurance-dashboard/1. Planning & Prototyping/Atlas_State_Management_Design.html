State & Data Lifecycle Design for Atlas

A dedicated companion to the Agent Architecture Plan §09. Atlas's state has to remain reconstructable across a renewal cycle that can run a year or more, and legally retrievable well past that — a fundamentally different permanence requirement than a case-workflow system whose state mostly matters while a case is open.

01 Why this needs its own document

The architecture plan's §09 makes the core claim: Atlas is closer to a ledger than a workflow tracker. Its “current” state is the latest slice of an append-only history that has to stay reconstructable at any prior point in time. Three things make this a harder problem than it looks:

It's explicit, not incidental. FR3.7 requires the system to “never overwrite a prior value silently — version changes and keep an audit trail.” §10.3 goes further: “historical KPI and risk-score values retain the weights/thresholds in effect when they were calculated ... so a later reweighting does not silently rewrite history.” That's not a general good-engineering-practice footnote — it's a named functional requirement with an auditor as a persona (§4.1) whose job depends on it holding.
The timescale is long and PRD-defined, not sprint-scoped. A policy's own inception–expiry window (typically annual, longer for project-length covers like CAR/EAR) is the natural unit of “current,” and §9.2 requires Atlas to run correctly in parallel with legacy trackers for at least one full renewal cycle per entity before that tracker retires. Claims can stay open even longer — §7.4's “Open vs Closed / Settlement time” tracking implies a claim can outlive the policy period it was filed against entirely.
Getting it wrong is invisible until an audit. A system that mutates a KPI or risk-score in place will look completely correct in every day-to-day view. The failure only surfaces when someone tries to reproduce a number from six months ago and it's silently different — exactly the audit-defensibility scenario the PRD's Executive Summary calls out as a primary business driver (“a defensible, audit-ready basis”).

02 The core pattern: valid-time and transaction-time

One modeling idea covers almost every requirement in §01: distinguish when something was true from when the system learned it. This is a well-established database pattern (bitemporal modeling); Atlas doesn't need the full academic version, but it needs both axes, not just one.

Valid time — the period during which a fact was actually true in the real world (a policy's own inception–expiry dates; the period a given sum-insured value applied). Transaction time — when Atlas itself recorded or corrected that fact (when a validator confirmed an extracted field; when a later correction was posted). These are different axes and can disagree: a broker might send a corrected schedule in July showing the sum insured was actually different back in March. Atlas needs to answer both “what was true in March” (valid time) and “what did we believe in March, versus what we believe now, and when did that change” (transaction time) — the second question is exactly what an auditor tracing a number back to source is asking.

Why a simple mutable-row-plus-change-log isn't quite enough: a change-log answers “what changed and when” but reconstructing “what the system would have shown a user on a given past date” means replaying every log entry up to that date — fragile, slow, and easy to get subtly wrong (e.g. around a config-weight change, per §10.3). Storing valid-time and transaction-time as first-class columns on the record itself (or as an explicit snapshot table, §06) makes that a direct query instead of a replay.

04 The retention clocks

Four different clocks govern how long something has to remain retrievable — conflating them is the easiest way to accidentally archive something too early.

Renewal-cycle clock ~1 policy term

A policy's own inception–expiry window. Typically annual across most §17.4 lines; potentially multi-year for project-length covers (CAR/EAR). Not a fixed PRD duration — it's whatever the placed policy's own term is.

Regulatory-retention clock unspecified — §13

“Retain documents and records per the Group records-retention policy” — almost certainly longer than one renewal cycle for financial/insurance records, but the PRD doesn't state a duration. Flagged in Open Questions.

Audit clock effectively permanent

Immutable, time-stamped log of every data change, validation action, export, and access event (§13). Doesn't prune during normal operation.

Migration clock 1 renewal cycle, one-time

§9.2's parallel-run requirement — a temporary, per-entity clock during onboarding: Atlas runs alongside the legacy tracker for at least one full renewal cycle before that tracker retires.

The rule that falls out of this: nothing gets archived or hard-deleted before both the renewal-cycle clock and the regulatory-retention clock have elapsed, whichever is longer — and the audit clock never triggers a deletion under normal operation at all.

05 A policy's lifecycle, end to end

timeline
    title A single policy's state, from placement to next renewal
    Placement and intake : Document uploaded and classified : Field Extraction runs, human validates : Posted as v1 by Enrichment and Posting
    Mid-term, months 2 to 10 : Endorsement changes a limit, v2 posted, v1 retained : Claim filed, independent lifecycle begins : Exclusion discovered, Contract Compliance version updates : Every KPI or score recompute writes a dated snapshot tagged to the config version live at the time
    Renewal window, day 90 to 30 pre-expiry : Renewal Pipeline KPI tracks lapse risk (Section 7.6) : Alerts fire at 90, 60, and 30 days
    Renewal : New policy period placed, new Policy and Coverage versions : Prior period's versions never deleted, still queryable for trend, audit, and any claim still open against it
    Ongoing : Regulatory-retention clock keeps running independently of renewal : Audit log entries from every step above remain permanent
Every stage after intake appends a new version or a new snapshot — nothing in this timeline overwrites what came before it.

06 Implementation recommendation

Three distinct storage patterns, matched to the three non-reference lifecycle categories in §03:

Versioned entities (Policy, Coverage, Premium, Asset, ExtractionField, Policy Exclusion, Third-Party Requirement)

A type-2 slowly-changing-dimension style table: each row carries valid_from / valid_to (the real-world period this value applied) and recorded_at (when Atlas learned it), with the current row simply the one where valid_to is open-ended. A correction to a past value doesn't touch the old row — it closes it and inserts a new one, so both “what we show today” and “what we showed on any past date” are direct queries, not replays.

Point-in-time snapshots (Risk Score, Coverage & Ratio KPIs, Contract Compliance status)

A separate, purely additive fact table keyed by (entity, metric, as_of_date, config_version_id). This is what §10.3 actually requires: a KPI value is meaningless without knowing which threshold/weight version produced it, so that foreign key is not optional. Recomputation writes a new row; it never updates an existing one.

Configuration versions (KPI thresholds, risk-score weights, risk-appetite thresholds)

Gap worth naming: §8.1 doesn't list a “Configuration Version” entity even though §10.3 requires exactly this — a dated, approved version of thresholds/weights that every snapshot above references. Recommend adding it explicitly to the data model rather than treating it as implicit inside Config Change-Control.

Append-only feeds (Document, FX Rate, News Item/Source) and the Audit log

These need no update path at all by design — every write is an insert. The Audit log in particular should be structurally incapable of an UPDATE or DELETE in normal operation (enforced at the database/permissions layer, not just by convention), since it's the record of last resort if any other layer's versioning is ever questioned.

07 How this differs from a case-workflow system

Without visibility into Sentinel's actual internals, the general shape of a third-party-onboarding workflow system is usually: a case opens, moves through review/approval stages, and closes — state matters most while the case is open, and once it resolves (approved, rejected, withdrawn), older intermediate states typically don't need to remain independently queryable and legally frozen forever. The system's job is mostly to get to a decision, then move on.

Atlas doesn't get to “move on” in that sense. A placed policy doesn't resolve to a terminal state — it stays “open” for its full term, accumulates mid-term changes, and every number computed against it during that time has to remain exactly reproducible indefinitely afterward. If Sentinel's state model is built around current case status, Atlas's has to be built around what was true, and what we believed, as of any date — which is a materially different default to design for, not just a longer retention setting on the same pattern.

08 Open questions

Mirroring the PRD's own §18 style — these need an answer from the sponsor/IT & Data Engineering before the storage design is final.

Regulatory-retention duration: §13 references “the Group records-retention policy” without stating a duration. Insurance/financial records commonly carry 6–7+ year minimums — needs confirming so the retention clock in §04 has a real number.
Configuration Version as a first-class entity: should §8.1's data model be updated to name this explicitly, given §10.3 already requires its behaviour?
Does the same versioning bar apply to News/Emerging-Risk Signals? FR6.9's confirm/dismiss is clearly versioned, but the PRD doesn't say whether dismissed signals are retained indefinitely or can be pruned once dismissed and no longer influencing the Risk Score.
Claim data ownership after policy retirement: §5.1 scopes claims as data-capture only (Atlas doesn't process claims) — confirm whether Atlas is expected to keep tracking an open claim's status after its policy period has fully lapsed and been superseded by a new placement.
